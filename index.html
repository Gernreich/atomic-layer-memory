Why the spacer

A close-packed layer has to nest in the hollows of the layer below, so two neighbouring layers can never sit on the same site. With B between every bit, 0 = A and 1 = C, and any bit string is an allowed stack: 00 becomes A B A, never A A.

Silicon and carbon

Silicon carbide already grows this way. Its Si–C bilayers sit on A, B or C sites, and its many polytypes (3C, 4H, 6H) differ only in stacking order. The figures in the panel use SiC's spacings: 0.308 nm between neighbouring atoms in a layer, and 0.252 nm per bilayer.

Reading from the side

Seen from the cut side face, the three sites sit at three different positions across the face: A at 0, B a third of the way along and C two thirds. An atomic force microscope tip run up the stack meets each layer's atom row at one of those three places, so the zig-zag it traces is the code. Read with AFM shows the tip doing that and decodes the trace.

After Drexler

Drexler's Engines of Creation and Nanosystems imagined machines that place atoms one at a time. Writing a stack like this needs exactly that: one layer at a time, each on a chosen site. Reading it back needs atomic resolution on the side face, which atomic force microscopes and electron microscopes both reach today.
